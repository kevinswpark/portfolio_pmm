Where to keep it

Keep your emergency fund in a high-yield savings account. The best ones pay more than 10 times the national average, and the money is still there when you need it.

  • Insured by the FDIC (banks) or NCUA (credit unions), up to $250,000
  • No monthly fee and no minimum balance to earn the rate
  • A rate that lasts, not a six-month promo
  • No direct deposit or monthly deposit requirement you can’t meet
  • Transfers to checking in one or two business days

    Step 6 of 8

    Decide how much to invest

    A common target is 15% of your pay before taxes for retirement, counting your employer’s match.

    Ways to invest

    Once you know how much, you pick how. These are the main options, from least effort to most.

    Target-date funds

    One fund holds a mix of stocks and bonds and gets more careful as its target year gets closer. It’s the default in many 401(k) plans.

    Lowest effort. Check the yearly fee, called the expense ratio, since it varies a lot.

    Index funds and ETFs

    One fund holds hundreds or thousands of companies by tracking a market index, so no single company can sink you.

    Low effort, wide spread, and yearly fees that are often under 0.10%.

    Robo-advisors

    An app asks about your goals, builds a portfolio of funds, and rebalances it for you.

    Low effort outside a 401(k). Expect a fee of about 0.25% a year on top of the funds’ own fees.

    Individual stocks

    You buy shares of one company at a time. It’s a good way to learn and a risky way to save.

    High effort and little spread unless you own many. Many people keep this to a small slice, under 10%.

    Why fees matter

    A 1% yearly fee sounds small. On $500 a month for 30 years at a 6% return, you’d end up with about $80,000 less than with a 0.05% fee.

    Investing on autopilot

    Putting the same amount in every payday, through ups and downs, means you buy more when prices drop. It also takes the guessing out.

    Rebalancing

    Once a year, move money back to your target mix. If stocks had a great year, that means trimming them.
